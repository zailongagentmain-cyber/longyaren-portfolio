Machine-Readable Dates in 2026: The Only On-Page Feature That Survived a Same-Search Test

Published 30 October 2026 · 12 min read · Long Yaren, SEO & GEO Specialist

The short version. In the strongest on-page study published for AI Overviews so far, fourteen page features were tested at once against each other and then corrected for multiple testing. Exactly one survived: a machine-readable date, worth +7.9 points of citation likelihood with a 95% interval from 2.9 to 12.9 (Underneath's v1.1 study, via OctoLink, 23 Oct 2026). Organization schema, BreadcrumbList schema and HTML tables showed no meaningful association at all — their intervals sit inside plus or minus five points. In the same update, question-form headings fell from +9.6 points to +3.5 points, with an interval from −0.3 to 7.1, which means they are no longer distinguishable from zero.

This article breaks down what the surviving signal actually is, what the date does and does not buy you, and how to implement it without turning it into a date-stamping gimmick.

What the same-search test actually did

Most AI Overview research compares a cited page against pages that never ranked. That design has a flaw: the cited page may differ from the comparison page in everything at once, including how well it ranks. Underneath's version 1.1 study, updated on 8 October 2026, changes the comparison to something much harder to fake (Underneath v1.1, via OctoLink, 23 Oct 2026).

It looked at 486 US searches that showed an AI Overview. For each search it fetched the pages ranking in the top ten, then compared the pages the overview actually cited against the pages the overview passed over — on that same results page. The comparison is therefore against competitors on that specific search, not against the web in general. Fourteen page features were entered into the model together, and the study reports a correction for having tested fourteen features at once. The reported source corpus is 3,096 AI Overview pages.

Position still came first, as it usually does: 41.7% of pages ranking one to three were cited, against 20.1% of pages at positions seven to ten, and within the same search, position accounted for 6.1% of the variation in which pages were cited (Underneath v1.1, via OctoLink, 23 Oct 2026).

Then the sobering part. The fourteen page features together added 2.5 points more. The study reports that 91.4% of the variation was left unexplained by anything it measured (Underneath v1.1, via OctoLink, 23 Oct 2026). This is the honest shape of the problem: on-page tuning is a second-order lever, and the field of AI Overview optimisation has been selling it as a first-order one.

The one feature that survived

After the correction for fourteen simultaneous tests, exactly one on-page feature still predicted citation: a machine-readable date, at +7.9 points with a 95% interval from 2.9 to 12.9 (Underneath v1.1, via OctoLink, 23 Oct 2026).

Two things in that table are worth slowing down on.

The first is what a machine-readable date is. It is a date a machine can parse without inferring it from prose: datePublished and dateModified in Article schema, an article:published_time or article:modified_time Open Graph property, or a <time datetime="..."> element. A visible line that reads "Updated on 30 October 2026" is not machine-readable on its own unless it is also expressed in one of those forms. Both halves matter: the machine-readable form makes the date parseable, the visible form makes the claim auditable by a reader who will check it.

The second is the question-form heading row. In version 1.0 it was worth +9.6 points; in version 1.1 it is +3.5 points with an interval spanning zero (Underneath v1.1, via OctoLink, 23 Oct 2026). If your content strategy rests on question-form headings because of the original study, that strategy needs re-testing against your own queries — the effect did not hold up under correction. This site uses descriptive headings rather than question-form ones, and the study is one of the reasons why.

Why it only works on informational queries

The date signal is not uniform. The same update reports +17.8 points for informational searches against +1.0 on commercial and transactional searches (Underneath v1.1, via OctoLink, 23 Oct 2026). Article schema shows the same split, +10.9 against +0.1.

That is not a quirk of the study; it matches what the age data shows independently. In a separate first-party study of 243 AI Overview citation events across 224 unique pages, harvested on 28 July 2026, 44.3% of citations on transactional queries could be dated at all, and those dateless citations were mostly product and landing pages — 48 citations pointing at product or landing pages and 22 pointing at videos (Blogging Titan, age analysis of 243 AI Overview citations, 2026).

The mechanism is straightforward. A transactional query is "buy X" or "X price" — the answer engine needs a price, a spec, a shipping policy, not a sense of how current the commentary is. A date carries no information there. An informational query is "how does X work" or "is X still true in 2026" — a date is exactly the disambiguating fact the answer needs, and a page that declares a machine-readable date lets the engine resolve "as of when" without guessing.

Do not read the intent split as "dates only matter for blogs." It means the date signal is worth your engineering time on pages whose queries are informational. For a product catalogue, the correct read of the same data is that a date is not your lever — the price, the availability and the specification are. Adding date markup to a product page because a study said dates work is applying the finding outside its stated scope.

Age is not the point — maintenance is

The instinct after reading a freshness study is to publish more. The age data says the opposite. In the 243-citation study, the median cited page was originally published 12.8 months before it was cited, with a mean of 24.2 months, and 33.3% of dateable citations were first published more than two years before being cited (Blogging Titan, age analysis of 243 AI Overview citations, 2026). Google cited a 2015 email marketing guide, a 2016 hosting roundup and a 2017 Reddit thread in July 2026. Publish date is not the filter.

What the same data does show is maintenance. Measured by the most recent touch — update date where one exists, publish date otherwise — the median cited page was 6.2 months old, roughly half its publish age (Blogging Titan, age analysis of 243 AI Overview citations, 2026). Among cited blog articles specifically, the median original age was 12.4 months against a median effective age of 5.3 months, and 54.4% of cited articles had been meaningfully refreshed after publication (Blogging Titan, age analysis of 243 AI Overview citations, 2026). Across all dateable cited pages, 46.9% had been refreshed after publication, 69.1% had been published or updated within the last 12 months, and 30.2% within the last 90 days.

That gap between publish age and effective age is what the study calls the refresh dividend, and it is the mechanism behind the surviving on-page feature. A machine-readable date is not a magic token. It is the receipt for work that already happened. It is only worth what the revision underneath it is worth.

The staleness penalty

A second first-party study, published alongside the age analysis, ran 20 Google searches, found an AI Overview on 18 of them, and compared the published and last-modified dates of 156 independent publisher pages, with dates present on 132 (Blogging Titan, content freshness and AI Overview citations: a 156-page study, 2026). Read the table carefully and the shape becomes clear. There is a freshness premium of almost nothing at the top: the cited group was last updated three weeks fresher on median, the two groups were equally old at first publish, and within six months they were effectively tied at 63% against 61%. About 22% of both groups were articles over a year old that had been updated in the last six months — slapping a new date on an old post is not a differentiator because everyone is already doing it.

The gap is at the stale end. Only 5.6% of cited pages had gone untouched for more than two years, against 9.3% of uncited pages. Very stale content was nearly twice as likely to be ignored. Stale pages were not absent from citations — more than one in twenty cited pages had not been touched in over two years — so age nudges the odds, it does not decide them.

The operational rule, in one line. Keep pages from crossing the two-year-stale line; refresh on a six-to-twelve-month cycle where the facts actually move; publish a truthful machine-readable date at the moment you do the work. Chase nothing faster — the data does not support a sub-three-month update cadence across a site.

Implementing a machine-readable date

Here is the minimum viable implementation. It is deliberately small, because the study says the effect is +7.9 points with a wide interval, not a switch.

1. Emit the date in JSON-LD

<script type="application/ld+json">
{
  "@type": "Article",
  "datePublished": "2026-10-30",
  "dateModified": "2026-10-30"
}
</script>

Keep datePublished stable for the life of the page. Change dateModified only when you genuinely revise the page — never as a routine refresh, and never on a scheduled calendar.

2. Mirror it in Open Graph

<meta property="article:published_time" content="2026-10-30">
<meta property="article:modified_time" content="2026-10-30">

This is how a consumer of the page, rather than a search engine, sees it. It also keeps your social cards honest: an OG card that shows a modified date should agree with the structured data.

3. Make it visible and parseable

Show a plain-language line near the top of the article — "Published 30 October 2026" and, when the page has genuinely been revised, "Updated on <date>" — and wrap the date itself in a <time datetime="2026-10-30"> element so that the visible text and the machine-readable value are the same fact stated twice. This article's own footer does exactly that.

4. Keep the two in sync

The highest-value implementation mistake is having a visible date that disagrees with the structured data. The age study dated pages using, in order of preference, schema.org datePublished and dateModified markup, article meta tags, visible byline and "updated on" dates, and platform timestamps (Blogging Titan, age analysis of 243 AI Overview citations, 2026). If your three sources disagree, you have taught the machine a different date than the one your reader sees.

5. Make the revision real

The 156-page study's own conclusion is that a fresh timestamp cannot explain who gets picked, and that a genuine revision that improves the answer is what counts, with the new date as a side effect of that revision rather than the cause of the citation (Blogging Titan, content freshness and AI Overview citations: a 156-page study, 2026). Every one of this site's articles carries a real dateModified for the same reason — a date that changes when the page changes is only credible if the page changes.

Three ways to misuse this signal

1. Date-bumping

Changing dateModified without changing content is the most common abuse of the one surviving on-page signal. The 156-page study found recently-updated pages distributed roughly equally between cited and uncited groups, which is what a non-differentiator looks like (Blogging Titan, content freshness and AI Overview citations: a 156-page study, 2026). The age study also notes that its definition of a meaningful refresh cannot distinguish an honest update from a date bump (Blogging Titan, age analysis of 243 AI Overview citations, 2026). You are not fooling the model, and you are not fooling the reader.

2. Deleting and republishing

The oldest cited pages in the age study kept their URLs and their accumulated authority and layered fresh data on top; the oldest editorial citation was a post first published in 2015, still cited in 2026 (Blogging Titan, age analysis of 243 AI Overview citations, 2026). Republishing under a new URL resets the clock you were trying to beat.

3. Treating a citation as a quotation

The Underneath update reports that only 21.0% of cited pages were the answer's first source, and that in 62.2% of cited pages, no passage that cites the page had at least 80% of its content words on the page (Underneath v1.1, via OctoLink, 23 Oct 2026). Being cited and being quoted are different outcomes. The same update notes that pages containing a table were 14.7 points more likely to have their passages absorbed, even though the table did nothing for the citation itself. If you are optimising for extraction, format for extraction; if you are optimising for being cited, the date is the only on-page feature with evidence behind it.

Why a date is not a retrieval strategy

One more piece of evidence, and it is the constraint that should govern how much you invest here. A study of two 90-day collections of answers to commercial questions in contrasting markets — 158,099 answers with 468,037 citations — found that page properties explain very little of the variation between pages of the same website, while the engine's own retrieval step explains far more: 32% against 2.8% for page properties (arXiv 2610.15033, 10 Oct 2026). The same work reports that every page ChatGPT cites is one its own search retrieved, and that the sub-queries the model rewrites rarely coincide with Google's or Bing's results.

That paper also draws a distinction that matters for how you read the date result. It reports that content matching — pages whose title and paragraphs match the questions people ask — was cited more, whereas FAQ blocks, structured data, summary boxes and page speed appeared effective only across websites rather than between pages of the same site (arXiv 2610.15033, 10 Oct 2026). If that pattern holds, the same page can win on one query and be ignored on another without anything on the page changing, which is exactly the volatility documented elsewhere in this portfolio.

How to size the investment. A machine-readable date is cheap to implement — an afternoon across a template — and the evidence says it is the only on-page feature with a surviving signal. It is not a substitute for being retrievable, and it is not a substitute for content that answers the question. Do the date properly, then spend the rest of your effort on the part that explains 32% of the variation.

FAQ

Does a machine-readable date really increase AI citations?

It is the only on-page feature in the most rigorous test published so far that survived a correction for fourteen simultaneous tests: +7.9 points with a 95% interval from 2.9 to 12.9, on 486 US searches showing an AI Overview (Underneath v1.1, via OctoLink, 23 Oct 2026). The effect was concentrated on informational queries at +17.8 points, against +1.0 on commercial and transactional searches.

What counts as a machine-readable date?

datePublished and dateModified in Article schema, the article:published_time and article:modified_time Open Graph properties, or a <time datetime="..."> element with a machine-parseable value. A human-readable date string with no parseable form is not machine-readable.

Should the date be visible on the page?

Yes, and it should agree with the structured data. The age study dated pages from schema markup first, then article meta tags, then visible byline and "updated on" dates (Blogging Titan, age analysis of 243 AI Overview citations, 2026). Three sources that disagree teach the machine a different date from the one the reader sees.

Does updating the date without changing the content help?

No. Recently-updated pages appear roughly equally in cited and uncited groups, so a fresh timestamp cannot explain who gets picked; a genuine revision that improves the answer is what counts, with the new date as a side effect (Blogging Titan, content freshness and AI Overview citations: a 156-page study, 2026).

How often should I update pages for AI search?

On a six-to-twelve-month cycle where the facts move, and never letting a page cross the two-year-stale mark. The cited-versus-uncited gap was almost nil in the first year of age and only opened up at the stale end, where pages untouched for over two years were nearly twice as likely to be uncited (Blogging Titan, content freshness and AI Overview citations: a 156-page study, 2026). The data does not support a blanket "update everything every three months" rule.

Does AI search only reward new content?

No. The median cited page in the 243-citation study was originally published 12.8 months earlier, with a mean of 24.2 months, and 33.3% of dateable citations were first published more than two years before being cited (Blogging Titan, age analysis of 243 AI Overview citations, 2026). What the cited pages share is recent maintenance, not recent publication.

Is schema markup still worth adding in 2026?

For readability and eligibility, yes. For citation leverage on its own, the evidence is weak: in the same-search test, Organization schema, BreadcrumbList schema and HTML tables showed no meaningful association with being cited, while Article schema split the same way the date did, +10.9 on informational searches against +0.1 on commercial and transactional ones (Underneath v1.1, via OctoLink, 23 Oct 2026). Put the date fields in your Article schema; do not buy schema as a citation strategy.

Should I put question-form headings on every page?

Re-test it before you bet on it. Question-form headings were worth +9.6 points in version 1.0 of the study and +3.5 points in version 1.1, with an interval from −0.3 to 7.1 — no longer distinguishable from zero (Underneath v1.1, via OctoLink, 23 Oct 2026).
