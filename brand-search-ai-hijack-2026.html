Brand Search AI Hijack in 2026: When Google Answers Your Brand Name Before Your Site Does

By Long Yaren · Published 30 October 2026 · 16 min read

Bottom line: the assumption that brand searches are safe ground has expired. As of 29 September 2026, AI Overviews appeared in 82.91% of US desktop search results for branded keywords, up from a July monthly average of 61.3% (Ahrefs, 2 October 2026). Worse for most brands, the answers are not built from your own pages: in the same study, 71.9% of AI Overviews on brand queries cited Wikipedia, and YouTube and LinkedIn followed. This article is about what that does to the way a brand is discovered, what the data does and does not prove, and the three layers you can still work on: the entity behind the name, the third-party sources Google is actually reading, and the first-party fact pages it can lift.

It deliberately does not repeat the rest of this site's advice about earning citations, and it does not repeat the click-loss diagnosis. The question here is narrower and sits upstream of both: when someone searches for your brand, whose description of your brand is the first thing they read?

The headline number, and the three different numbers hiding inside it

Almost every article about this finding quotes one figure: 83%. Almost none of them tell you that this is a single-day reading and that the monthly average for the same month was meaningfully lower. The distinction is not pedantry. If you build a business case on the headline and your own logs show something closer to the monthly average, you will conclude that your brand is uniquely lucky or uniquely unlucky, and you will draw the wrong conclusion about whether anything needs to change.

Ahrefs analysed 232.8 million US desktop Google search-result crawls from July to September 2026, made up of 92.5 million branded-keyword crawls and 140.2 million non-branded-keyword crawls (Ahrefs, 2 October 2026). From that dataset come three different published numbers, and they measure different things.

September 2026 monthly average 73.3% The number to plan against. It is an average across the month, which smooths the days when the feature does not fire.
Independent sample, 1,000 US brand keywords, checked three times in October 2026 82.5% A different team, a different keyword list, a different month, and it lands near the headline rather than the monthly average (SE Visible, 22 October 2026).

Source: Ahrefs, 2 October 2026 and SE Visible, 22 October 2026. The honest summary is that in US desktop results during autumn 2026, something between seven in ten and five in six branded searches now show an AI Overview. That is a large shift. It is not a single precise constant, and anyone who tells you it is has picked one of these three numbers and dropped the other two.

Brand search was not immune to AI Overviews in 2025 — it was simply less affected than everything else, and that gap has now largely closed.

A limit worth stating plainly: both datasets are US desktop. Mobile, other countries and other query types are not measured by these studies. SE Visible's own study put Germany at 40.4% for brand-name searches, roughly half the US rate (SE Visible, 22 October 2026), which is enough to establish that the US figure does not travel automatically.

The harbour is gone: branded and non-branded searches have converged

Until recently, brand search had a specific property that made it the safest traffic on the site: the query was so unambiguous that Google had no reason to summarise anything. A user typing your company name wants your company. There was nothing to compare, nothing to disambiguate, nothing to answer except you. The AI Overview had nothing to add, so it did not fire.

That reasoning has stopped holding. In November 2025 the same research team found AI Overviews were far more common for unbranded queries than branded ones — a gap of 12.2 percentage points in July 2026, down to just 5 points by September (Ahrefs, 2 October 2026). For the first time, an AI Overview on a branded query is an ordinary event rather than an exception.

The reason matters for what you do about it. An AI Overview does not need to be informative to be triggered — it needs to be possible. A brand today has an entity footprint: a Wikipedia article, a YouTube channel, a LinkedIn company page, an app store listing, a business profile, review pages, press coverage. Google can assemble a description of you from those pages whether or not your own website participates at all.

What this means strategically: the question is no longer "how do I make Google show an AI Overview for my brand". The question is "which of my pages and which third-party pages are the raw material for the summary Google shows for my brand". Those are different problems, and only the second one is still open.

76.2% of brand AI Overviews sit in position one

Prevalence alone does not tell you how bad it is. An AI Overview at the bottom of the first page is an inconvenience. An AI Overview at the top, replacing your own brand link, is a structural change to how your brand is discovered. The Ahrefs study separates the two, and the answer is not reassuring.

The team took exact-name queries for 100 brands — selected from Interbrand's Best Global Brands 2025 list — and checked them at 14 weekly checkpoints between July and September 2026. This is a separate and much smaller sample than the 232.8 million-crawl dataset above, and it covers bare brand names rather than every query containing a brand; the authors are explicit about that distinction.

  • By 30 September, 63 of the 100 brand-name queries had an AI Overview, compared with just 5 of 98 usable results at the 1 July checkpoint.
  • Of those 63 AI Overviews, 48 appeared in position one — 76.2% — with an average position of 1.49.

Source: Ahrefs, 2 October 2026. A rise from five brands to sixty-three in one quarter is the kind of step change that gets noticed late, because the leading indicator is not a traffic chart but a manual check. And an average position of 1.49 means the AI Overview is not an occasional guest at the top of the branded SERP — it is the default occupant of the position where the brand's own homepage used to sit.

This is the specific mechanism of the hijack, and it is worth naming precisely, because it is not the same as the traffic collapse described elsewhere on this site. The AI Overview CTR collapse is about how many people click after the summary renders. This is about who occupies the slot before the click decision exists: the summary is in position one, and your homepage is no longer the first thing the page shows. The two effects compound, and they have different remedies.

Where the answer comes from: Wikipedia, YouTube, LinkedIn

Here is the finding that changes the shape of the problem. In the same 100-brand panel, Ahrefs recorded which external domains the AI Overviews cited. The distribution is not led by brands' own websites.

External domain citedAI Overviews citing itShare
Wikipedia 41 71.9%
YouTube 22 38.6%
LinkedIn 13 22.8%
Yahoo Finance 9 15.8%
Facebook 6 10.5%
Walmart 4 7.0%

Source: Ahrefs, 2 October 2026. Wikipedia alone leads at 71.9% of the AI Overviews that carry external citations — the same authors round this to "almost 72% of external citations" — and it does so for a reason that should be obvious once stated: Wikipedia is the most link-dense, most internally consistent description of a well-known entity on the open web, which makes it the cheapest and most reliable input for a summariser that has to be right.

Read that list as an instruction, not as a complaint. YouTube and LinkedIn are platforms that make it much harder to control a brand's own narrative than an owned domain does. A brand that has spent a decade earning links and building its own pages discovers that, for a branded query, none of that work is what gets read.

Scope note: these are shares of external citations, i.e. citations pointing at domains other than the brand's own site. They are not share of clicks, and they are not a measure of accuracy — an AI Overview citing Wikipedia is a statement about which page Google read, not about whether the answer was right. The SE Visible study puts 61% of AI Overview citations on brand-side platforms — brand website, app stores, business profile, social and video platforms, review sites — so the split between "your own pages" and "the wider web" is narrower than the Wikipedia number alone suggests, but the point stands: brand-site pages are a minority of the raw material.

What the answer is not doing

There is a competitive-scraping fear attached to this topic, and it deserves a straight answer rather than either alarm or dismissal. The Ahrefs data is reassuring in a specific, bounded way.

  • Of the AI Overviews where the full text was visible, only 2 of 58 — 3.4% — explicitly discussed competitors. Pepsi's answer named Coca-Cola in its description of the cola wars; Intel's named TSMC and NVIDIA.
  • Looking at the citations themselves, only 1 verified competitor-owned website appeared across the 57 available source lists.

Source: Ahrefs, 2 October 2026. The AI Overviews in this sample were, in the authors' words, at least smart enough to avoid sourcing information from competing brands. For the 100 largest brands, the hijack is happening without competitor injection.

Two caveats prevent that from being good news. The sample is the world's largest brands — the population with the best-resourced Wikipedia articles and the most consistent entity footprint. A mid-market brand is a different case, and the sample says nothing about whether the pattern holds below the top 100. And 3.4% is a rate on a panel of 58 visible answers: read it as "not the mechanism", not as "cannot happen".

An independent read on the same question

The Ahrefs findings come from a vendor that sells AI-visibility tooling, so the obvious question is whether the same numbers show up when a different team with a different keyword list goes looking. They do, and the disagreements between the two studies are as informative as the agreement.

SE Visible, the brand-visibility product of SE Ranking, published a study on 22 October 2026 by Yulia Deda. It checked 1,000 US brand keywords and 1,000 German brand keywords across 17 industries, checking each keyword three times on desktop during October 2026 (SE Visible, 22 October 2026). The US runs returned an AI Overview for 827, 827 and 820 keywords — an average of 82.5%. For 756 keywords (75.6%) it appeared in all three runs, 870 (87%) showed one at least once, and 11.4% switched between checks. Germany sat at 40.4%.

Three things in that paragraph are strategically important, and none of them are about the headline number.

First, 11.4% of brand queries flip between checks. If your monitoring tool takes one reading a month, a meaningful slice of your brand queries will show an AI Overview one month and not the next. That is not measurement error; it is the feature being unstable at the query level. It also means single-screenshot audits of brand SERPs are close to worthless as a trend measurement.

Second, the biggest brands are the least affected. Keywords with fewer than 50,000 US searches showed AI Overviews 85.2% of the time, against 63.3% for keywords with over 1 million searches. The most searched brands are the ones with the most entrenched, well-supplied entity footprints, and those are the hardest for a summariser to surprise. If you are a large brand, your exposure is lower than the headline suggests; if you are a mid-market brand, it is higher.

Third, the pages Google cites for a brand are not the pages you would expect. This is the finding that should change your page-level priorities.

Brand-site page typeShare of brand-site URLs in the organic top 10Share of brand-site citations inside AI Overviews
Homepage 71.1% 36.8%
Product, service and category pages 6.1% 22.9%
About and company information pages 3.8% 11.7%

Source: SE Visible, 22 October 2026. The homepage dominates organic results and is a minority of AI Overview citations. Product, service and category pages and About pages, which barely register in the top ten, take a far larger share of the citations. Put together, 62.2% of the brand pages cited inside AI Overviews did not rank in the top ten for that same query — rising to 92.6% for product pages and 84.6% for About pages, with the homepage the outlier at 16.5%.

At the domain level, overlap between AI Overview citations and the organic top ten was 98.3%. The domain usually matched; the page often did not. And an average AI Overview cited 2.57 brand pages, against 1.72 brand pages in the organic top ten for the same query. Google is reading more of your site than your rank tracker shows you.

The trap: a rank report saying "brand keyword: position 1" can be completely accurate while the AI Overview summarises your pricing from a support article, your founding date from a 2021 About page, and your locations from a third-party directory. Your rank tracker is not broken. It is measuring a different thing from what the user sees first.

Why brand queries behave differently from every other query

Brand queries are unusual in a way that changes the tactics, so it is worth being precise about the difference.

For a non-branded query — "best running shoes for flat feet", "how to reduce server costs" — the AI Overview has to do real work: synthesise many pages into an answer to a question with no single right answer. That is a retrieval task, and retrieval tasks reward being the best-retrieved source for a sub-question. This is the world the rest of GEO advice is built for, and it is covered on this site in the complete GEO guide and in how to get cited in AI Overviews.

For a brand query, the AI Overview is not a retrieval task. It is a description task. Google is assembling a short factual profile of a named entity, and the profile comes from whatever pages state those facts most plainly: the Wikipedia article, the official video, the company page, the app store listing, the review page, the business profile. These are largely structured, largely factual, largely third-party pages — which is precisely why they are so heavily cited.

Two consequences follow directly, and they are the spine of the rest of this article.

Consequence one: "make my page more citable" does not move brand queries much. A brand's own page is rarely the best source for a factual profile of the brand, because a brand's own page is written to sell. The pricing page has caveats, the About page has mission language, the homepage has a hero section and a logo. The Wikipedia article has a infobox with the founding year, the headquarters, the industry and the key people, and it has been checked by people whose job is to keep it correct. That is why it wins 71.9% of the external citations in the Ahrefs panel.

Consequence two: what you can still change is the content of the third-party pages, and the factual density of your own. You cannot make Wikipedia say something different by optimising your site. You can make sure the Wikipedia article about your company contains the facts you would want a stranger to read, that it does not contain a stale founding date or a wrong headquarters, and that the video on your YouTube channel opens with a twenty-second description of what the product is. And you can make sure that your own pricing page, specification page and location pages state the same facts in the same plain language — because those pages are now in the running to be cited, and they are the only ones in the running that you fully control.

Where the numbers stop: neither study establishes that any of this changes traffic or revenue. The SE Visible study says so itself — it measures AI Overview presence and citation patterns, not answer accuracy, traffic or conversions. And the Ahrefs panel of 100 brands is drawn from the Interbrand Best Global Brands 2025 list, so it describes the behaviour of very large, very well-documented entities. Both limits are real, and both are reasons to audit your own brand queries rather than to extrapolate.

Three layers you can still work on

Given the mechanism above, the response has three layers, and they are ordered by how much of the answer they control. This is the priority order I use with clients, and it is deliberately different from a content-volume playbook.

Layer 1 — the entity: make the brand's facts exist in structured, neutral form

Before anything else, the brand needs to exist as an entity in the places an answer engine can read without your involvement. Wikipedia, Wikidata, the official LinkedIn page, the app store listing, the Google Business Profile, the Crunchbase and industry directory entries. Each of these is a place where a fact about you can be stated plainly, and each is a place where a summariser can lift it.

The work here is unglamorous and mostly editorial: check that the founding year, headquarters, ownership, product categories, headcount band, key people and notable dates are correct and consistent across all of them. Inconsistency is the failure mode that matters, because a summary assembled from two conflicting sources is where your brand's story becomes ambiguous. The mechanics of building entity authority are covered in entity SEO and knowledge graph authority; this article's point is narrower — on a branded query, entity consistency is the ranking layer.

Layer 2 — the third-party sources: own the facts you do not own the page for

The SE Visible breakdown of where citations come from is the most actionable framing I have seen on this, because it separates the sources you can publish on from the sources you can only respond on.

  • 40.5% of citations come from places the brand manages outright: the brand website (33.8%), app store listings (4%) and Google Business Profile (2.7%).
  • 20.8% come from places the brand can publish on or respond in: social and video platforms (18.1%, mostly YouTube) and review sites (2.7%).
  • The remainder comes from pages the brand does not control at all.

Source: SE Visible, 22 October 2026. So roughly three in five citations land somewhere you can act on directly. For the part you cannot control — Wikipedia, a directory, a news article — the action is not SEO. It is correction, sourcing and consistency: make sure the facts are right, make sure they are sourced, and where a platform offers a mechanism to correct or dispute a claim, use it.

Layer 3 — first-party fact pages: your own page, written as a fact source

This is the layer most teams skip, because it does not look like content marketing and it does not look like link building. The finding that makes it worth doing is the page-type table above: product, service and category pages take 22.9% of brand-site citations against 6.1% of top-ten share, and About pages take 11.7% against 3.8%.

A first-party fact page is a page whose job is to be quoted: one canonical statement of a fact, in plain language, with a date on it and the entity named explicitly. Specification tables with real numbers. A pricing page with the actual current price. A location page per branch with address, hours and phone. A comparison page that names both sides. A "how it works" page that explains the product in two hundred words a model can lift into a paragraph.

The reason this works on brand queries specifically is that brand answers are factual, and facts have a canonical form somewhere. If your pricing exists in three slightly different versions across three pages, and one of those pages is the one Google reads, you have handed the answer engine an ambiguity. If there is one page where the price, stated plainly, is dated and canonical, then that page wins the quotation for the price — and it wins it on the one surface where you have full control.

The reframe: on a branded query, your website is not competing with the AI Overview for attention. It is competing with Wikipedia, YouTube and LinkedIn to be the best available source of a fact. That is a different competition, and it is winnable with editing rather than with budget.

The 90-minute audit: what to capture before you change anything

Everything above is contingent on knowing what Google currently says about your brand and what it reads to say it. This is the audit, and it is deliberately manual, because the tooling that automates it is the tooling whose coverage you are trying to verify.

Step 1 — build the query list (20 minutes)

Start from Search Console's query report filtered to brand terms, and add the variants a human would actually type: the bare name, the name plus a product, the name plus "pricing", "login", "reviews", "near me", "alternatives", the name plus a city. Twenty to forty queries is enough to be representative. Write them down, because you will re-run this list monthly.

Step 2 — capture the answer and its sources (30 minutes)

For each query, record: did an AI Overview appear, what did it say, what position was it in, and which URLs were cited. Screenshot rather than copy — AI Overviews change wording, and a screenshot is what you will want when you argue with a stakeholder about what Google claimed. Check each query at least three times, on different days, because 11.4% of brand queries switched between checks in the SE Visible sample. A signed-out or private window reduces personalisation noise.

Step 3 — diff against your own top ten (20 minutes)

For each query with an AI Overview, list the organic top ten. Mark which pages of yours are there and which pages of yours are cited in the summary. The gap between the two lists is your work list. Pages cited but not ranking are assets you already have and are not exploiting. Pages ranking but not cited are the ones most likely to be rewritten as fact pages.

Step 4 — audit the third-party layer (20 minutes)

For the external domains that showed up most often, check whether the facts they state about you are correct, current and sourced. Wikipedia and Wikidata first, then LinkedIn, YouTube, app stores and your business profile. Log any discrepancy, however small — a wrong founding year in an infobox is the kind of error that propagates directly into an AI Overview.

What to record, and what not to record: log the cited URL set, the wording of the answer, the position and the date. Do not try to compute a "brand visibility score" from it and do not compare it to a vendor's benchmark number for a different brand. The useful signal is the month-over-month change in your own citation set and in the accuracy of your own facts, nothing else.

A 30 / 60 / 90-day plan

First 30 days — measure and correct

  • Run the audit above and turn it into a standing list of twenty to forty queries with a monthly re-check date.
  • Correct every factual discrepancy you found in the third-party layer. This is the highest-leverage work available and it costs no budget.
  • Establish one canonical version of each fact that matters: current price, key specifications, locations and hours, founding and leadership details.
  • Check that your Wikipedia and Wikidata entries, if they exist, state the same facts in the same way as your own pages.

Days 31 to 60 — publish the fact layer

  • Publish or refresh the first-party fact pages the audit identified as missing: specification, pricing, location, comparison, and a short plain-language description of what the product does.
  • Give every one of those pages an explicit last-updated date in machine-readable form, and make sure the date is honest — see machine-readable dates and AI citations for why an unreliable date is worse than no date.
  • Audit your YouTube channel and LinkedIn page as owned publication surfaces, because they are cited more often than most brands treat them.
  • Re-run the audit and compare citation sets month over month.

Days 61 to 90 — measure entity consistency and widen

  • Run a consistency sweep across every third-party surface that describes you, looking for conflicts in numbers, dates and names rather than for rankings.
  • Publish one asset that contains original, checkable data about your category — the kind of thing a model can lift a number from, and the kind of thing competitors cannot copy.
  • Add a brand-visibility line to your reporting, using the citation set rather than a score, and put it next to your organic brand-query performance.
  • Re-test the hypothesis: if brand citations from third-party sources are now correct and yours are cited more often, the hijack has been partially reversed. If nothing moved, the next question is entity recognition itself, and the entity SEO guide is the next document to read.

And one thing to keep in view throughout: this is not a traffic recovery plan. Brand queries were never the highest-volume traffic on most sites, and they were never the most profitable — a user who already knows your name converts better than a user who does not. What is at stake on brand queries is accuracy of the first description, which affects how you are described in every AI answer that mentions you afterwards, in every assistant, not only Google. Frequently asked questions

Is 83% of brand searches really showing an AI Overview?

It depends which number you mean. The 83% headline is a single-day reading from 29 September 2026 in Ahrefs' US desktop crawl data, where the measured rate was 82.91%. The monthly average for September was 73.3%, up from 65.1% in August and 61.3% in July. An independent SE Visible study of 1,000 US brand keywords put the October average at 82.5%. The direction is not in doubt; the exact figure depends on the day, the sample and the country. What is true everywhere in that range is that brand search is no longer exempt.

Do AI Overviews on brand queries really hurt traffic?

The click data comes from a different dataset and a different mechanism than the prevalence data. Ahrefs' separate click study measured a 58% lower click-through rate for position-one pages when an AI Overview is present, on US desktop Search Console data across 300,000 keywords. That is a population-level correlation, not a measurement of any single site's traffic. It holds for informational queries, which is the population the study sampled, so a brand query whose users click through anyway will not behave the same way. The full diagnostic is in the AI Overview CTR collapse guide.

Can my own website still be the source of the brand answer?

Yes, but not through the homepage, and not as a share you would recognise from a rank tracker. SE Visible found that homepages made up 71.1% of brand-site URLs in the organic top ten but only 36.8% of brand-site citations inside AI Overviews, while product, service and category pages rose to 22.9% and About pages to 11.7%. In total 62.2% of cited brand pages did not rank in the top ten for the same query. The citation is available to you; it is just not coming from the page you are proudest of.

Are competitors being injected into my brand's AI answer?

Rarely, in the sample that was measured. In Ahrefs' panel of 100 brands, only 2 of 58 visible AI Overviews explicitly discussed competitors, and only one competitor-owned website appeared across 57 available source lists. The larger risk is not that a rival is named; it is that a platform you do not control — Wikipedia, YouTube, LinkedIn — supplies the facts your brand is described with. Note also that the sample is the world's largest brands, so a mid-market brand should not assume the same restraint.

How is this different from the US, and does it apply outside the US?

Do not assume the US numbers travel. SE Visible measured 40.4% for German brand searches in the same study, roughly half the US rate, and 68.5% of cited German brand URLs sat outside the top ten. Both the Ahrefs and SE Visible studies are US desktop snapshots from 2026, so mobile results, other countries and later months may differ. Check your own market before you budget against these figures.

Should I worry about the AI Overview being wrong about my brand?

That is a separate risk from this one, and it is the one most worth watching. Both studies here measure presence and citation patterns, not accuracy — the SE Visible authors say so explicitly. The pragmatic response is the layer 2 work: make sure the third-party pages Google reads state correct, current, sourced facts, because those are the inputs. If the answer is still wrong after that, you have a correction problem rather than a ranking problem, and correction paths are platform-specific.

What should I do in the first 30 days?

Run the audit before you change anything: capture your brand queries, record every source each AI Overview cites, and compare that list with your organic top ten. Then fix the facts that are wrong or missing on the third-party sources you actually appear in, because the summary is assembled from those pages, not from your site. Only then build or refresh first-party fact pages that state pricing, specification, location and comparison facts in a form a model can lift.

Is this a content problem or a data problem?

Mostly a data problem, which is why content volume is the wrong response. The AI Overview for a brand query is assembled from factual statements about the brand, and the statements live on a small set of pages: a Wikipedia article, a video, a company page, a store listing, a directory. Publishing more blog posts does not change the content of any of them. Making those pages state accurate, current, plainly-worded facts does. The content question only reappears at layer 3, where you write pages that are explicitly built to be quoted.
