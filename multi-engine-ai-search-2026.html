Multi-Engine AI Search Optimization in 2026: ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, AI Overviews

By Long Yaren · SEO & GEO Specialist · Updated 22 October 2026

Bottom line: There is no single "AI search" to optimize for. ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Google AI Overviews and Microsoft Copilot run different crawlers, build different indexes, and reward different page structures. Treating them as one channel is the single most expensive mistake in 2026 — it is why 78% of teams now call attribution their number one problem. The winning move in 2026 is to split your work by engine: fix crawler permissions per bot, write content that satisfies each engine's extraction logic, and measure each engine separately.

Ten years of SEO worked because there was one index. Google was the index; Bing was a smaller copy; everyone else borrowed from one or both. That era is over, and not gradually. In 2026, the answer engines that actually send you traffic draw from sources that barely overlap with each other. A page cited in Perplexity may never appear in ChatGPT. A page that ranks on Google's traditional results gets cited in an AI Overview only about a third of the time. Optimizing "for AI" as a single undifferentiated target now means averaging your effort across five machines that are each rewarding different things.

This guide is a per-engine operating manual. It covers which bot grants which permission, what each engine's citation logic actually rewards, how to adapt one page into five engine-friendly variants, how to measure the five separately, and the five misjudgments that cost teams the most visibility. It assumes you already have the basics — an indexable site, an accurate sitemap, and pages that render without JavaScript. If you do not, fix that first: no amount of engine-specific craft survives a page that AI crawlers cannot parse.

1. Why one-size-fits-all AI SEO stopped working

The strategy that dominated 2024 and 2025 was simple: make your content extractable, put the answer up top, add structured data, earn mentions, and watch AI referrals arrive. That advice was not wrong. It was incomplete in a way that becomes expensive the moment you split your reporting by engine.

Three facts about 2026 make per-engine work mandatory rather than optional.

First, the citation pools barely overlap. Analysis of multi-engine citation data in 2026 found roughly 11% overlap in cited domains across engine families. Even Google's own two surfaces do not agree with each other: AI Overview citations overlap Google's traditional top ten only around 17%. This means a page being invisible on one engine tells you nothing about the others, and a strategy that only tracks one engine is measuring a sample of one.

Second, the mechanics differ at every layer. Each engine has its own crawler with its own purpose, its own index, its own retrieval layer, its own query expansion behaviour, and its own citation display. Perplexity answers cite 16.35 sources on average and pull roughly 46.7% of its top-ten citations from Reddit, a community-sourced validation pattern. ChatGPT answers average 6.88 sources and lean on encyclopedic and professional-network sources. Google AI Overviews draw from Google's own index plus video. These are not cosmetic differences — they are different selection functions, and content that wins one can be ignored by another.

The operative reframe. Stop asking "how do I rank in AI search" and start asking "which engine am I trying to win, and what does that engine consider a good source?" Every downstream decision — which bot to allow, which paragraph to rewrite, which metric to report — gets easier once the engine is named.

Third, the economics have already flipped. Global monthly AI-referred visits reached 7.707 billion in the period measured by Similarweb, up 117.4% year over year. Adobe's 2025 holiday-season data showed AI-referred traffic converting 31% better, spending 45% longer on site and browsing 13% more pages. And a peer-reviewed 2026 study covering 973 e-commerce sites, more than 20 billion dollars in revenue and over 50,000 ChatGPT-attributed transactions found that LLM referral conversion lands between paid social and traditional organic channels — not above everything. The demand is real and growing fast. Which engine it comes from is no longer a detail.

2. The permission layer: which bot grants which right

Before an engine can cite you, it has to be allowed to read you, and the permission is granted per bot, not per vendor. This is the layer most teams get wrong, because most teams think in vendors ("block OpenAI") rather than in purposes ("block training, allow search").

The durable classification framework has three categories, and it holds across every major vendor:

CategoryWhat it doesTypical user agentsYour decision
SearchBuilds an index used to answer later queriesOAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, Claude-SearchBot, Googlebot, bingbotAllow. This is the citation path.
TrainingCollects corpora for model training or fine-tuningGPTBot, ClaudeBot, CCBot, BytespiderUsually block. No traffic comes back.
AgentActs on behalf of a live person: opens pages, reads them, fills formsChatGPT-User, Claude-User, Perplexity-User, Google-AgentAllow for docs, e-commerce and B2B. Consider for paid publisher content.

Two structural points about this table matter more than the list itself.

robots.txt is a request, not a lock. It is the only channel that is officially documented across vendors, and it works — but enforcement happens at the edge. Cloudflare has stated plainly that robots.txt cannot identify who is crawling or why, and cannot stop crawlers that ignore it. If your CDN or WAF blocks a request before it reaches your origin, the request never appears in your server logs at all. "I do not see it in my logs" and "it never came" are different statements, and conflating them will send you debugging the wrong layer.

Once a crawler has a named group, it ignores the wildcard group. Under RFC 9309, a crawler that finds a group naming it obeys only that group. So if your robots.txt contains User-agent: * with Disallow: /wp-json/ and then later a named group for a search bot with only Allow: /, that bot is now allowed to fetch /wp-json/. The common bug is the reverse one: a blanket User-agent: * with Disallow: / that silently blocks every search bot including Googlebot, unless each one gets an explicit named group.

Verification is not optional. A user-agent string is self-asserted text and is trivially spoofed. Across a study of 174 Cloudflare-protected sites, only about 10–30% of AI bot requests could be verified as genuine. OpenAI, Perplexity, Anthropic and Google all publish IP ranges or verification endpoints, and Web Bot Auth — a signed crawler identity standard with AWS WAF support since November 2025 — is the emerging mechanism. Until you verify, treat unverified "AI" traffic as an unknown cohort and exclude it from every report.

3. GPTBot versus OAI-SearchBot: the distinction that decides everything

If you read one section of this guide, read this one. OpenAI runs four distinct agents, and conflating them produces either lost visibility or unwanted training inclusion. The two that matter most are GPTBot and OAI-SearchBot, and they do opposite-value jobs.

GPTBot is the training crawler. It collects public web content that may be used to train OpenAI's generative AI foundation models. It returns nothing: no link, no referral, no impression. Disallowing it is an opt-out from future training and nothing else — ChatGPT search is unaffected.

OAI-SearchBot is the search crawler. It discovers and indexes pages so ChatGPT's search feature can surface them with a link. OpenAI's documentation is unambiguous about the cost of getting this wrong: sites that opt out of OAI-SearchBot "will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers, though can still appear as navigational links." In other words, blocking it does not just reduce your citations — it removes your pages from the answer body while leaving a bare link that nobody clicks. This is the same silent-failure pattern that loses rankings: you are present in the data and absent from the outcome.

The other two OpenAI agents complete the picture. ChatGPT-User is a user-triggered fetcher — it opens a page when a person asks ChatGPT or a Custom GPT to read it. Because the request originates from a human action, OpenAI states that robots.txt rules may not apply, which means a Disallow aimed at ChatGPT-User is a request rather than a guarantee; real enforcement needs a server or CDN control. OAI-AdsBot validates pages submitted as ads on ChatGPT and touches nothing else.

The four decisions, separately. OpenAI itself frames it this way: each setting is independent of the others. You can allow OAI-SearchBot so you appear in ChatGPT search while disallowing GPTBot so your content does not feed training. The industry-standard configuration for most commercial sites is exactly that — search allowed, training denied, agent fetches allowed for documentation and product pages. Two operational details: when both bots are allowed, OpenAI may reuse the results of a single crawl for both purposes rather than crawling twice; and changes to robots.txt can take roughly 24 hours to take effect in ChatGPT search, so judge the outcome on a delay, not immediately.

For log filtering, match on the bot token rather than the full string, because OpenAI versions its agents and the version number changes — GPTBot/1.4 and OAI-SearchBot/1.4 at the time of writing. Note also that OAI-SearchBot may send a variant with an inserted robots.txt marker when fetching your robots file; matching on the bare token catches both variants. A regex on the substring "GPT" alone will lump three different agents into one bucket and destroy any per-engine analysis you try to build afterwards.

4. The rest of the map: Perplexity, Google, Microsoft, Anthropic

OpenAI's bot list is the one most teams know. The other engines have their own distinctions, and each one changes what you should write.

Perplexity: two agents, opposite robots.txt semantics

Perplexity runs PerplexityBot, which builds and updates the index behind its cited answers and respects robots.txt, and Perplexity-User, the on-demand fetcher that pulls a page live when a user's question requires it. Perplexity's documentation has historically stated that user-triggered fetches generally ignore robots.txt, on the reasoning that a person asked for the page. That position has shifted under public pressure and current published policy says both agents respect a robots.txt block — but the practical lesson survives either way: if you need a hard block on user-triggered fetching, robots.txt alone is not your control. Use a server or CDN rule.

Perplexity's important property for SEO is that it always cites. Every answer carries a numbered source panel, which means it is one of the few AI surfaces where citations convert directly into referral clicks. It also returns roughly three times as many sources per answer as ChatGPT, over a pool of 16.35 on average, which means Perplexity is the least zero-sum of the major engines for citation share. If you can only build depth on one engine, Perplexity has the best citation-to-effort ratio — a fact that also means its citation pool is the most crowded.

Google: one index, three surfaces, and one misread token

Google's AI Overviews, AI Mode and Gemini all draw from Google's own index, crawled by Googlebot. AI Overviews appear above the traditional results; AI Mode is a conversational experience that fans the query out into parallel subqueries; Gemini is the standalone assistant with its own surface list, including in-vehicle queries and Notebook sessions. The practical consequence of that shared pool is that Gemini citation work and AI Overview citation work are the same investment, and both are inherited from classic Google ranking.

Google-Extended is the token most teams misread. It is a product-control setting covering Gemini training and grounding uses. It is not a separate HTTP crawler, and — per Google's own documentation — it does not affect Google Search indexing or ranking. Only standard Googlebot controls such as nosnippet affect whether your content appears in AI Overviews. The common claim that "blocking Google-Extended removes you from AI Overviews" is wrong, and acting on it means you blocked training and kept search, or believed you had protected search when you had not protected anything at all. Google has also stated that there is no separate AI index, that llms.txt is not used in Search, and that no special markup is required for AI features.

Microsoft Copilot: the Bing index

Copilot draws web-grounded answers from Bing's index, which makes Bing Webmaster Tools directly relevant and makes your Bing standing a Copilot asset. Copilot is embedded in Windows, Edge, Outlook and Microsoft 365, which gives it an enterprise distribution channel none of the others have. Bing has historically been more receptive to mid-tier domains than Google, so for smaller brands Copilot is often the cheapest engine to win. There are also two Copilot-specific controls worth knowing: NOARCHIVE keeps a page out of Copilot answers and NOCACHE limits Copilot to title, URL and snippet.

Anthropic and the rest

Anthropic documents three agents: ClaudeBot for training, Claude-SearchBot for search discovery, and Claude-User for live user-directed fetches. Claude's web search backend is closely tied to Brave Search, and Claude's results track Brave's index. This produces a striking property: a page that is strong in Bing may be strong in ChatGPT, while a page that is strong in Brave may be strong in Claude, and the two sets rarely coincide. Claude is also the most conservative citer — it prefers fewer, higher-authority sources per answer — which means winning there usually requires being one of the most-cited pages for the topic elsewhere on the web.

The general pattern: Google's surfaces share one index, ChatGPT's search leans on Bing plus its own crawler, Copilot leans on Bing, Claude leans on Brave, and Perplexity runs its own. Your visibility is the union of the indexes you happen to be in, and no single action reaches all of them.

5. How each engine decides what to cite

Access is a permission, not a citation. Even a perfectly crawlable page has to win a selection process. These processes are proprietary, so what follows is a description of observed behaviour and published vendor guidance, not a ranking formula. But the observed behaviours are consistent enough to plan against.

Perplexity: freshness plus citation density

Perplexity rewards specific, current, densely-cited passages. It extracts quotable chunks rather than whole pages, which means each paragraph must stand alone when quoted. Practically: lead with the answer, use question-shaped headings that match how people phrase queries, put concrete numbers in sentences ("73% of users prefer X" beats "most users prefer X"), and start paragraphs with a noun rather than a pronoun so a lifted fragment still makes sense. Outbound citations to primary sources matter here more than on any other engine — the system rewards pages that themselves cite evidence. Freshness is weighted heavily, and Perplexity's willingness to surface hours-old content means recency signals get read literally.

AI Overviews and AI Mode: extractability from Google's own index

Because AI Overviews draw candidates from Google's ranked results, classic SEO remains the foundation — but the extraction layer has its own preferences. Google's own guidance for generative AI features emphasizes text that exists in the page, internal links that make content discoverable, and structured data that matches visible content. Empirically, AI Overviews cite pages that are close to featured-snippet-shaped: a 40-to-60-word direct answer paragraph near the top, question-shaped headings, and short declarative statements that can be lifted as a complete sentence. The March 2026 core update reinforced this by pushing the ecosystem toward demonstrably useful, entity-anchored content, and AI Overview behaviour tracked it.

One high-leverage detail from 2026 research: pages ranking for a cluster of related subqueries were dramatically more likely to be cited than pages ranking for the head query alone. Google's query fan-out rewrites one question into many parallel retrievals — equivalence, follow-up, generalisation, specification, clarification — and a page that covers the cluster can match several of them while a page that covers only the head query matches one. Topic clusters are not an authority-building nicety here; they are how you get retrieved more than once per prompt.

ChatGPT: entity consistency and structured knowledge

ChatGPT's live retrieval layer sits on top of a training-derived model of the world, and the two reward different things. Live retrieval rewards extractability; the model's prior rewards entity clarity. In practice, ChatGPT leans on sources that are unambiguous about what they are and who wrote them: definition-first openings, a clear named author with a stated role, consistent entity naming, and pages that other authoritative sources have already cited. Wikipedia and professional networks are heavily represented in ChatGPT's citation pool for a reason — they are maximally unambiguous. If your brand's description differs across your own site, your LinkedIn page, your Wikipedia entry and your directory listings, the engine is resolving four different entities, and the fix is upstream of any content rewrite.

Gemini: multimodal and visual parseability

Gemini inherits Google's index and adds a multimodal layer. That makes image and video parseability part of the citation surface rather than a marketing afterthought: descriptive alt text, captions that carry the substantive facts, transcripts, and charts whose content is also present as text. A comparison delivered only as a rendered image is invisible to extraction, no matter how good the image is. Google's own AI content guidance notes that its AI answers can draw on what is said about products and services across the web — including blogs, videos and forum discussions — which means the visual and conversational surfaces are part of the citation pool, not decoration.

Copilot: Bing authority plus verifiability

Copilot's answers and citations depend on content Bing can clearly interpret and verify, per Bing's own webmaster guidelines, which ask for facts and definitions written out, key statements that do not depend on implied context, key information placed near the top of the page, one topic per URL, and consistent entity naming. Copilot rewards pages that read like verified reference material rather than marketing copy. Because it mirrors the Bing index, a Bing-strength strategy and a Copilot strategy are largely the same strategy, which makes Copilot the lowest marginal-cost engine to improve for most teams already doing traditional SEO.

6. Adapting one page into five engine-ready versions

The practical version of all of this is a rewriting discipline. You are not producing five unrelated articles; you are producing one canonical page with engine-targeted components. The rule that makes this work is to keep a single canonical URL and a single indexable body, and to build the engine-specific surfaces as structured, extractable elements inside it.

  1. Put a 40-to-60 word direct answer immediately under the H1. This is the highest-leverage single element for AI Overviews and Perplexity and it costs nothing else. The paragraph must stand alone if quoted out of context: no pronouns referring back, no "as we discussed above".
  2. Repeat the answer as a self-contained block at the start of each major section. Retrieval is chunk-based and platform behaviour shows a strong positional bias toward the opening portion of a document — frequently only the first 30%. A section that begins with context and reaches the point three paragraphs later may never be quoted, because the chunk that reaches the point never gets selected.
  3. Render comparisons as HTML tables, never as images. Tables are the single most extractable structure there is, and they carry the same information to a text extractor that a screenshot does not. A comparison chart that only exists as a PNG is invisible to every AI crawler that does not execute JavaScript or read pixels.
  4. Anchor every entity explicitly. Define the category, define your product, define the relationship between them, using consistent names. Where a third-party encyclopaedic entry exists for you or for the category, make sure the description matches it closely. This is what makes ChatGPT's entity resolution converge on one version of you.
  5. Show your sources with dates. Perplexity and ChatGPT both reward pages that cite primary sources with dates. Every quantitative claim in this article carries a source and a publication date for exactly this reason — it is not decoration, it is a citation signal and it lets a reader verify without leaving the page.
  6. Keep a visible dateModified and update the substance, not just the stamp. Research on 7,683 pages found that 75% of LLM-cited pages had been updated within the past year, and framed the mechanism explicitly: the freshness that LLMs reward is manufactured by updating, not by publishing. Refreshing an existing page that already ranks for the topic is cheaper than commissioning a new post on the same theme.
  7. Build a FAQ block that answers real questions in full sentences. The question in the heading is the retrieval key; the answer paragraph is the extractable unit. Do not split answers into bullets if a sentence will do — a fragment cannot be lifted cleanly.
  8. Make captions carry facts. For Gemini and multimodal surfaces, put the substantive information in the caption and alt text, not just in the filename.

Two caveats. First, the widely repeated finding that Q&A formatting itself raises a page's influence weight was not supported by 2026 analysis of 21,143 citations — the effect is real for retrieval but not as a standalone citation lever. Second, a 2026 controlled experiment of 1,885 pages adding JSON-LD against 4,000 matched controls found no significant citation uplift on any platform. Keep structured data because it aids entity disambiguation and correct interpretation, and because it may win ties; do not sell it as a citation switch.

7. Measuring each engine separately

If you have read the companion piece on AI citation tracking, you already have the panel design: fixed prompts, repeated runs, per-engine mention rate, answer position, and source analysis. The per-engine dimension is what makes it operational rather than descriptive, and it has to be carried all the way down into analytics, not just into the prompt panel.

Split referrers properly. GA4's native AI Assistant channel launched on 13 May 2026 and automatically identifies referrers such as chatgpt.com, gemini.google.com and claude.ai under a single ai-assistant medium. Two things about it limit its usefulness for this work. It applies only to new data and does not backfill, so any year-on-year comparison across that date contains a discontinuity you must label explicitly. And it collapses all assistants into one row, which is exactly the averaging problem this article is about.

The fix is a custom channel group in GA4, built on a session-source regular expression, with the AI group ordered above the Referral group so it matches first. A workable pattern is .*chatgpt\.com.*|.*perplexity\.ai.*|.*claude\.ai.*|.*gemini\.google\.com.*|.*copilot\.microsoft\.com.* — one group per engine, not one for all. The two advantages over the native channel are that it can be applied retrospectively to historical data, and it produces per-engine rows. One warning from Google's own example: a pattern like ^.*ai sweeps in every domain ending in ".ai", which is a great many software companies. Match on specific domains instead.

Align this with the revenue model, not against it. The attribution framework from the companion ROI article should carry the same per-engine split: first-touch, assisted-position-based and last-touch run on the same cohort, reporting a range rather than a point estimate, because AI referrals frequently complete research in one short session and convert later through a brand search or a direct visit. Running the same three models per engine is what turns a channel report into a defensible one.

Measure the pipeline, not just the outcome. Per-engine measurement should have three layers, and they are separated in time. The access layer is leading: crawler hits, crawl depth, status codes, duplicate-crawl rate, recrawl interval — per bot. The citation layer is mid-term: mention rate, citation share, answer position, quoted-verbatim rate, paraphrase rate. The revenue layer is lagging: AI-referred sessions, assisted conversions, attributed revenue, retention of AI-sourced customers. Reporting the leading layer as if it were the lagging layer is the first and most expensive sin of GEO measurement, and it is what makes AI traffic look like a failure when the pipeline is simply early.

Watch the three-way split in the data. Across the study of 174 Cloudflare-protected sites, AI traffic separated into fetches, tagged clicks and what the researchers called dark clicks — sessions where the AI referrer is present but the interaction is invisible to standard analytics. Per-engine analysis is more reliable than pooled analysis here precisely because dark clicks are unevenly distributed: an engine that strips the referrer entirely produces sessions that land in Direct, and only the engine-level breakdown makes the leak visible as a shape rather than as noise.

Keep the prompt panel per engine and per version. Record which engine, which model version, which prompt, what mention rate, what position, what sources were co-cited. Because cited domains churn 40–60% month to month, a single run is noise. Twenty fixed prompts run ten times per engine per month is a workable budget. If you can only afford one run, cut the prompt count and raise the repetition — one wide run produces a number you will later be tempted to trust.

Build the comparison you actually present. The output table should have one row per engine and columns for access rate, mention rate, citation share, average position, referral sessions, referral conversion rate, and assisted revenue. Two findings usually appear immediately. The first is that AI-referred conversion differs by engine rather than sitting at one industry-average multiple. The second is that the engine with the highest mention rate is often not the engine with the highest revenue, which is exactly why "AI traffic" reported as a single line is useless for allocating budget.

8. Five misjudgments that quietly delete your visibility

Misjudgment 1: treating "not cited" as "not crawled"

This is the most common and most expensive error, and it sends you to edit content when the real problem is a robots.txt rule, a CDN challenge, a redirect chain, or a page that never left the origin. The diagnostic order matters. First check access: is the search-class bot for that engine hitting your URL at all, with a 200, in your server logs? If it is not hitting at all, no content change will matter. If it is hitting but only your homepage, you have a crawl-depth or crawl-path problem, which is an internal-linking and rendering problem. Only when access is confirmed healthy should you move to the citation layer and ask whether the content is extractable, entity-clear, fresh enough, and authoritative.

There is a second-order trap here. Because AI crawlers generally do not execute JavaScript, a page whose FAQ, price table or specification exists only after client-side rendering is invisible to nearly every AI crawler except Google's family. If your access logs show the bot fetching the URL and your page still never gets cited, check whether the content was in the HTML the bot received. In one 2026 analysis of more than 500 million GPTBot fetches there was no evidence of JavaScript execution, and independent research by two other teams confirmed that GPTBot, ClaudeBot and PerplexityBot treat JavaScript as text rather than running it.

Misjudgment 2: blocking GPTBot and accidentally blocking OAI-SearchBot

Almost every "we blocked AI crawlers" rule is written by pattern rather than by purpose. Block GPTBot and you are fine — you excluded training and kept search. Block OAI-SearchBot and you removed yourself from ChatGPT search answers while leaving only navigational links. Block both, or use a wildcard, and you have removed yourself from the citation path of several engines at once. The two errors are wildly asymmetric in cost, and the one that gets written by accident is the expensive one.

The 2026 Cloudflare default-policy flip makes this worse in a way that is not obvious. Under the defaults that took effect on 15 September 2026, training and agent crawlers are blocked by default on pages carrying ads while search crawlers remain allowed, and mixed-purpose or undeclared crawlers are judged by the strictest applicable rule. The old "block AI bots" toggle was migrated to a "disallow AI training" setting rather than a full block — but mixed-purpose crawlers including Googlebot, Applebot and Bingbot are evaluated under the strictest rule, so choosing the wrong category can take down Google itself. The lesson is not "never block". It is that every rule needs a written reason attached to it, per bot, and the settings panel is not where you make that decision.

Misjudgment 3: assuming blocked Google-Extended means blocked AI Overviews

This one propagates through SEO blogs faster than through vendor documentation, and it is simply wrong. Google-Extended governs Gemini training and grounding uses. It does not affect Google Search indexing or ranking, and it is not the control that governs AI Overviews — that is Googlebot with standard directives such as nosnippet. A site that believed the myth and blocked Google-Extended may have lost Gemini grounding while gaining nothing, or may have gained nothing while believing it had protected its search presence. Check the actual policy against the vendor's own documentation, and treat aggregator blogs as a lead rather than a source.

Misjudgment 4: reading bot traffic as AI traffic, and AI traffic as revenue

Three different quantities get collapsed into one in most dashboards. Crawl fetches are not visits. Visits are not citations. Citations are not conversions. A dashboard that reports "AI traffic" by adding crawler requests to referral sessions will show explosive growth the moment a training crawler recrawls, and every number in it will be wrong. The Cloudflare data in 2026 showed bot traffic exceeding human traffic for the first time, with more than half of AI crawler requests being repeat fetches of unchanged pages — a pattern that inflates any fetches-based metric indefinitely.

The correct separation is mechanical: bot requests filtered by verified identity live in the access layer; human sessions filtered by referrer live in the analytics layer; citations live in the prompt panel; revenue lives in the attribution model. Four layers, four reports, one shared set of bot tokens and one shared definition of "AI". The moment you merge them, every downstream decision becomes uninterpretable.

Misjudgment 5: assuming one page can win every engine with one structure

This is the misjudgment the whole article exists to correct. The intuition is reasonable — a good answer-first page should work everywhere — and it is partially true. A direct answer paragraph helps on every engine. But the differentiators are real: Perplexity's 16-source pool rewards citation density and freshness, Google rewards index position and cluster coverage across a fan-out of subqueries, ChatGPT rewards entity unambiguity and structured knowledge, Gemini adds a multimodal layer, and Copilot rewards verifiability in the Bing index. A page optimized hard for Perplexity — dense quotable statistics, heavy outbound citation, aggressive freshness — will often underperform on AI Overviews, where the constraint is ranking position within Google's index rather than passage quality.

The resolution is not five pages. It is one canonical page whose components are tuned per engine: the answer block for extraction, the table for structure, the FAQ for query matching, the entity block for resolution, the citations and dates for trust, the captions for multimodal parsing, and the internal linking for cluster coverage. Same URL, same body, different emphasis. If you genuinely serve different audiences per engine — a documentation site and a consumer brand — then split the audience, not the URL. Canonical duplication is a citation liability in every engine's extraction logic.

9. A 90-day multi-engine operating plan

Here is a sequence that works, with the reasoning for the order.

Days 1–15: fix permissions and instrument access. Write a plain-language table with one row per bot — agent, vendor, category (search, training, agent), current decision, business reason, verification method — and reconcile it against what is actually deployed in robots.txt, the CDN, the WAF and any bot-management rule. Pay special attention to Cloudflare's AI Crawl Control, since its defaults changed in September 2026, and to named-group semantics in robots.txt. Then set up server-side logging with at least 90 days of retention, grouped by bot token, with status codes, byte counts, request path and recrawl interval. Confirm that each search-class bot receives a 200 and a full HTML payload on your key commercial pages.

Days 16–30: build the per-engine baseline. Define 20–40 prompts drawn from actual buyer questions, run them against each engine repeatedly, and record mention rate, answer position, co-cited sources, and sentiment. Segment by engine. This baseline is what makes the next 60 days interpretable — without it, you cannot distinguish a change that worked from a month of citation churn, which at 40–60% monthly domain turnover is a serious risk of false conclusions.

Days 31–60: fix the pages, once, for all engines. Prioritise by commercial intent, not by traffic. On each priority page: direct answer block under the H1, self-contained section openers, HTML comparison tables, an entity paragraph with consistent naming, a FAQ block in full sentences, visible dates with genuine updates, primary-source citations with dates, and captions carrying facts. Do this once. Resist building engine-specific landing pages; you want one canonical asset that five engines can each find a use for.

Days 61–90: distribute, measure, and reallocate. Put the per-engine GA4 channel groups in place so referrals land in separate rows, run the attribution models per engine on the same cohort, and produce the one-row-per-engine comparison table described earlier. Then reallocate effort toward the engines with the best revenue-per-unit-of-effort, not the best mention rate. Re-run the panel monthly and compare against the baseline, with the explicit caveat that a single month's movement is not a trend.

One habit that compounds. Keep a decision log: which page, which component, which engine, which hypothesis, which result. Because the citation pool churns monthly, most of what looks like an effect in a single month is noise. The teams that get better at this are not the ones with more tactics — they are the ones that can prove which tactic moved which number.

Frequently asked questions

Which AI engine should I optimize for first?

Start with the engine that already has commercial traffic or the strongest commercial fit, not the one with the biggest audience. ChatGPT and Perplexity tend to be the strongest referral engines, Perplexity because every answer cites and clicks convert, and ChatGPT because of its scale. Copilot is often the cheapest to win if you already have Bing footing, because it draws from the same index. The honest answer is that you should not pick one — the whole argument of this guide is that the picking is the mistake — but if you must sequence, sequence by measured return per engine-hour, and start with permission fixes everywhere before writing anything.

Do I need to write different content for each engine?

You need different emphasis inside one canonical page, not different pages. The shared components — the answer block, the tables, the entity paragraph, the FAQ, the citations with dates — serve every engine. What differs is where you spend the effort: Perplexity wants passage density and outbound citations, Google wants cluster coverage and ranking position, ChatGPT wants entity unambiguity, Gemini wants parseable visuals and captions, Copilot wants verifiable reference-style statements. Producing genuinely separate pages per engine creates duplicate-content problems and splits your own authority, which is a cost that shows up in every engine's extraction logic.

Is it worth blocking AI crawlers at all?

Only with a reason written down per bot. Blocking a training crawler is a legitimate business decision for content you consider proprietary. Blocking a search crawler removes you from the citation path of that engine — for OpenAI, the documented consequence is that your pages will not appear in ChatGPT search answers, only as navigational links. The 2026 default policies make accidental blocking easier than ever, particularly for mixed-purpose crawlers, which is why the sensible practice is an explicit machine-visitor clause table reviewed quarterly rather than a set of ad-hoc rules accumulated over three years.

Does llms.txt help?

Google's official material on generative AI features and AI optimization states that there is no separate AI index and that llms.txt is not used in Search. It is inexpensive to publish and harmless, and some non-Google consumers may read it, but there is no published evidence that it produces citations, and it should not be presented in a business case as a lever. The same demotion applies to schema markup as a citation strategy: keep it accurate for entity disambiguation and interpretation, and stop selling it as a citation switch.

How do I know if my per-engine split is actually working?

You need a baseline and repetition. Run the fixed prompt panel per engine several times per month, record mention rate, citation share and answer position, and compare against the same panel from two months earlier. Remember that 40–60% of cited domains can change month to month, so a single month's improvement is not a trend and not evidence. And keep the access layer in the same report: if the crawler hit rate for a given engine is flat, no content change in that engine's direction will show up, and chasing a citation metric on an engine that has stopped crawling you is a waste of a quarter.

Should I optimise for AI Overviews and Google Search separately?

No — and this is the clearest case in the set. AI Overviews draw candidates from Google's own ranked results, so classic Google SEO is the foundation and the AI layer is the extraction behaviour on top of it. Google has said publicly that no special markup is required for its AI features and that it does not need separate optimisation; the honest reading is that ranking well is the optimisation, and answer-engine formatting is the lift. The one genuinely distinct investment is topic-cluster coverage, because Google's query fan-out retrieves a page once per matching subquery, and a page that covers the cluster gets more retrieval attempts than one that covers only the head term.

How often do these rules change?

Often enough to matter and rarely enough to be worth a standing team. In the twelve months to October 2026 we saw Cloudflare flip its default AI crawler policy in September, OpenAI settle the relative semantics of its four agents, Google expand Search Console with a Generative AI report in June, and GA4 ship a native AI Assistant channel in May. The practical response is not constant monitoring — it is a quarterly review of the machine-visitor clause table and a monthly re-read of each vendor's crawler documentation, which is short, official, and stable enough to rely on over aggregator articles.

10. The one thing to take away

The competitive advantage in 2026 is not knowing more AI tricks. It is having decided, per engine, what you are allowed to give, what you are optimising to give back, and how you will know whether it worked. Teams that treat AI search as one undifferentiated channel will keep averaging their results across five machines that reward different things, and they will keep losing to teams that simply named the engine before they acted.

Start with permissions, because that is where the cheapest wins are still sitting unclaimed. Then measure per engine, because you cannot improve a number you are not reporting separately. Then write once, with the components that satisfy all five. Everything else is refinement.
